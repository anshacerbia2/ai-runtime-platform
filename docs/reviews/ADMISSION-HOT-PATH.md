# M2 admission hot-path measurement — 28 September 2026

This is a **local diagnostic**, not a production capacity result or approval to change transaction isolation. The reproducible harness is `npm run benchmark:admission` in [benchmark-admission.mjs](../../scripts/benchmark-admission.mjs). It calls the M1 admission service/repository against PostgreSQL, creating isolated applications, connections, profiles, and budget accounts; each case issues 100 unique admissions without provider calls. The harness removes its own rows after every case. A post-run query found zero benchmark applications, connections, executions, budgets, or rate windows.

Environment: Windows workstation, Node v24.11.1, PostgreSQL 15.5, Prisma/pg adapter, pool max **6**, statement timeout **5 s**. Each case uses one budget account per application. `shards=1` directs all 100 admissions to one application/connection/account; `shards=8` spreads them evenly across eight independent sets. p95/p99 are nearest-rank values in milliseconds. `BEGIN` counts include transactions retried internally by admission. Pool acquisition includes connection overhead. Lock-statement duration includes query execution and network round trip; PostgreSQL lock wait events were sampled every 10 ms and can miss short waits.

At concurrency 8–24, the observed `BEGIN` count exceeded committed admissions several-fold, even when work was spread across eight applications. PostgreSQL documents that a sequential scan in a Serializable transaction requires a relation-level predicate lock and can increase serialization failures. The temporary Read Committed result is promising but cannot be shipped as a one-line change. Operator management can change application/connection limits and routing policy without taking the admission advisory locks. Those races, plus shared quota-group changes and cancel/completion interleavings, need a lock-order and linearization review with deterministic tests before relaxing isolation. The next decision is whether to make all relevant writers participate in a narrower explicit-lock protocol, or retain Serializable and reduce broad predicate reads/retry amplification with measured SQL/index or permit-row changes. No Redis shedder or raw-SQL rewrite is justified by this single local run.

## Policy-change race found during review

A deterministic local PostgreSQL experiment held the route advisory lock after admission had read the application policy. While admission waited, an operator transaction disabled the application and committed; after the lock was released, admission still committed an accepted execution. The experiment reproduced twice with the **current Serializable** implementation. This is not a PostgreSQL isolation violation: the transactions overlap, so admission may be ordered before the disable even when its commit happens later. Management does not participate in the admission advisory-lock protocol. The experiment's stronger assertion (that any earlier disable commit must reject the overlapping admission) failed, so it was kept outside the integration suite rather than presented as a passing regression test.

At the time of the race review, gateway claim rechecked the primary connection and credential before dispatch but did not recheck application status. Thus an accepted execution could reach provider dispatch after an application was disabled. The policy decision needed before changing isolation was whether disable must prevent all _new_ admission/claims after it takes effect, and whether already-running work continues or is explicitly cancelled. A strict boundary would require PostgreSQL coordination between admission and management before policy reads, with a defined lock order, plus a claim-time application check; a change to Read Committed should be assessed only after deterministic races and capacity/accounting tests cover that protocol. No production isolation or dispatch behavior had been changed by that review.

Recommended contract: successful disable closes future admission and claim decisions through a database-enforced ordering point. Work whose admission or claim already crossed that point may finish; disabling does not silently cancel an active provider call. An emergency stop is a separate cancel/fence operation. This defines the boundary by transaction order rather than by the wall-clock order in which two overlapping requests return. Connection and binding revocation require the same explicit treatment before the isolation change is reconsidered.

## Policy-fencing follow-up before P2

Admission now holds PostgreSQL `FOR SHARE` locks on the application, selected connection, and eligible binding until commit; operator updates to those rows must wait. A disable that commits first is observed by admission and denied. Gateway claim takes corresponding locks and rechecks application, connection, binding, and central credential before creating a provider invocation. A later fallback attempt rechecks the application and fallback route before creating its invocation. A deterministic integration test covers both lock orders for application, connection, and binding revocation, including Serializable abort/retry; gateway tests cover revocation between admission and claim and between primary claim and fallback. Already-running primary provider work is not cancelled by disable.

The admission transaction remained **Serializable at this point**. These tests established the revocation boundary, not the safety of Read Committed for shared quota-group changes, concurrent profile/budget management, capacity counts, or accounting interleavings. No hot-path optimization was shipped in this policy-fencing follow-up.

Two full local benchmark runs after the policy locks both accepted every request. At 24 workers/eight shards, admission p95 was **7290 ms** with 570 transaction starts, then **4519 ms** with 635 starts, versus **3118 ms** and 568 starts in the earlier matched Serializable run. At one worker, p95 was 42 ms and 25 ms. The change did not remove retry amplification, and these variable local timings do not isolate how much of the difference comes from row locks rather than workstation/database conditions. The PostgreSQL lock-wait sampler observed only advisory waits in those 24-worker/eight-shard cases; the harness does not time policy row-lock statements separately. Treat performance as an open P2 issue, not as a validated speedup or production SLO. ## P2 decision and implementation

Admission now uses **Read Committed** with explicit, ordered database locks for every mutable invariant it consumes. It locks the application and selected profile alias, connection, and binding rows; acquires the sorted application and route advisory scopes; then locks budget accounts in ID order. Application and connection updates already conflict with the policy-row locks. Connection management now locks the connection and its sorted old/new route scopes, so moving a connection into or out of a shared quota group cannot overtake a capacity decision on another group member. Budget management locks the account before reading exposure, so a limit change revalidates committed held and posted units. Management, accounting, and other repository transactions remain Serializable.

Deterministic PostgreSQL races now cover both lock orders for application, connection, and binding revocation, plus profile-alias disable, a budget-limit change queued behind a reservation, and shared quota-group reconfiguration ordered before capacity admission. In the admission-first cases, admission commits and the policy change follows it. If the policy change owns the ordering point first, the later admission observes the new state. This is the agreed transaction-order contract; it does not turn disable into implicit cancellation of already-running provider work.

Two additional candidate runs also committed exactly 100 transactions for 100 admissions in every scenario. Their 24-worker/eight-shard p95 values were 141.3 ms and 174.9 ms, bracketing the instrumented run's 155.6 ms. The result removes the measured retry amplification rather than relying on one unusually fast sample.

In the instrumented candidate run, capacity-query p95 was 0.7–2.2 ms, policy-row-lock p95 was 0.5–2.3 ms, rate-window p95 was 0.5–2.0 ms, and budget-lock p95 was 0.4–2.5 ms. At 24 workers/eight shards, pool acquisition reached 78.7 ms p95 while PostgreSQL recorded only two lock-wait samples. At one hot shard, the route advisory statement reached 39.6 ms p95, which is expected serialization for one application/connection quota scope. These measurements do not justify a Redis admission shedder, permit-row subsystem, raw-SQL rewrite, or speculative indexes. Those changes would add another correctness surface while the measured database statements are small; production-scale history and deployment load may justify revisiting indexes separately.
